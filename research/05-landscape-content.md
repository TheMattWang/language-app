**The pattern:** every tool that genuinely forces spoken production and gives any kind of feedback (Duolingo Video Call, Speak, Langua, TalkPal, Quazel, Univerbal, Babbel Speak/Live, italki) is **cloud-dependent by construction** — they all route audio through a hosted ASR/LLM/TTS pipeline. Every tool that works offline (Anki, downloaded Pimsleur audio) either does no production at all (Anki) or forces production with **no feedback loop** (Pimsleur speaks at you, never listens). Peninsular-specific content exists as a first-class course option in exactly two places found here (Pimsleur, Babbel), both of which are the least interactive/adaptive tools on the list. I did not find, even in general knowledge, a shipping on-device/fully-offline tool that both forces free spoken production *and* scores/responds to it — that combination appears to not exist as a shipped consumer product.

### What's genuinely unserved

The specific gap is the intersection of three properties, not any one of them alone: **(1)** spoken production that is actually *required* to proceed (not optional, not recognition-dressed-as-speaking), **(2)** a feedback loop that reacts to what you actually said — not a scripted "good job" — for pronunciation, register (vosotros/distinción), and content correctness, and **(3)** works with zero network dependency. Nothing surveyed here hits all three: the cloud AI-conversation apps hit (1) and (2) but fail (3) by architecture (their whole value prop is a large cloud LLM+ASR); Pimsleur hits (1) and (3) but explicitly not (2) (no listening at all); nothing found offers a Peninsular-dialect-locked adaptive conversation partner at all, offline or online — Peninsular support in the market is currently limited to non-adaptive fixed-course content (Pimsleur, Babbel). That's the honest niche: a real-time, offline, dialect-locked, listens-and-responds spoken-production partner. It's a narrow niche because the reason nobody ships it is a real engineering constraint (small on-device models are unreliable Spanish-content authors, hence this project's own decision to source content rather than generate it) — not because the demand doesn't exist.

## 2. Offline content sourcing (bundled, no network)

### Tatoeba

Sentence-pair corpus, community-contributed, with per-sentence audio contributed by volunteers. Weekly exports (Sat 06:30 UTC) as `.tar.bz2`, tab-separated: `sentences.csv` (id, lang, text), `links.csv` (translation pairs), `sentences_detailed.csv` (+ username/date), plus a separate `sentences_with_audio.csv` and audio file bundle. [Downloads](https://tatoeba.org/en/downloads)

- **License:** mixed per-sentence. Bulk of text is **CC BY 2.0 FR** (attribution required); a subset is explicitly **CC0 1.0**. Audio license is set per-contributor and listed on each audio file's page — cannot assume CC0/CC-BY blanket for audio. [Downloads page](https://tatoeba.org/en/downloads) states this explicitly.
- **Size:** page does not publish exact bytes; historically the full multi-language sentence dump is in the ~300-400MB range compressed (UNVERIFIED — could not confirm current size, did not want to download blind).
- **Filtering to Peninsular:** Tatoeba has no dialect/region tag for Spanish sentences — it's just `lang=spa`. There's no way to isolate Peninsular vs. Latin American sentences from metadata alone; would need lexical/vosotros heuristics (e.g., regex for `vosotros`, `-áis`/`-éis` verb endings, `vale`, `tío/tía` as filler) to bias a subset, or manual curation. Treat Tatoeba as a **supplementary example-sentence pool for vocabulary**, not a dialect-pure corpus.
- **Acquisition:** `curl -O https://downloads.tatoeba.org/exports/sentences.tar.bz2` and `curl -O https://downloads.tatoeba.org/exports/links.tar.bz2`, or use the per-language custom export tool at https://tatoeba.org/en/downloads for `spa` only to shrink the file before download.

### OpenSubtitles / OPUS es corpora

OPUS hosts an `OpenSubtitles` collection (subtitle text mined from opensubtitles.org, sentence-aligned across language pairs) — this is the corpus the well-known hermitdave and other frequency lists are built from. I could not load the OPUS corpus page directly this session (repeated 404s on both `opus.nlpl.eu/OpenSubtitles-v2018.php` and the corpus/version URL — likely a routing/JS quirk in the fetch, not proof the corpus is gone; OPUS OpenSubtitles is a long-standing, well-known resource). **UNVERIFIED by direct fetch this session** — treat the specifics below as recalled/prior knowledge, confirm before bundling:
- **Content:** monolingual Spanish subtitle lines (`es` side of `OpenSubtitles/v2018` or later `v2024`), or parallel en-es / other pairs, sentence-per-line, no speaker/dialect metadata.
- **Licensing reality:** this is the important catch. OPUS republishes subtitle text scraped from opensubtitles.org; the underlying subtitles are typically **fan-translated copyrighted dialogue** (film/TV scripts), not rights-cleared text. OPUS's own terms say the corpus is provided for **research purposes**; opensubtitles.org's own ToS restricts commercial redistribution. There is **no clean permissive license** on the sentence content itself — you're relying on the "facts/short-phrase, transformative, research-use" gray zone, which is exactly why hermitdave's *derived frequency counts* (word + count, not the sentences) are the safer thing to bundle, not the raw subtitle text. Do not ship raw OpenSubtitles sentence text as in-app example dialogue in a shipped product without a lawyer's sign-off; using only the frequency-count derivative is markedly safer.
- **Size:** the Spanish monolingual OpenSubtitles dump is large (multi-GB raw, hundreds of MB compressed) — not worth bundling raw for this use case.
- **Filtering to Peninsular:** no region metadata; subtitles are a mix of Spain-dubbed and Latin-American-dubbed releases with no split. Not filterable.
- **Verdict:** skip bundling raw OPUS/OpenSubtitles text. Only use it indirectly via the FrequencyWords derivative (below), which is explicitly relicensed CC-BY-SA-4.0 by hermitdave.

### Common Voice Spanish

Mozilla Common Voice `es` is a crowdsourced read-speech dataset (short prompted sentences + volunteer recordings), released under **CC0 1.0** (public domain dedication) for both the audio clips and transcripts — this is Mozilla's standard, stated license for all Common Voice releases. [commonvoice.mozilla.org](https://commonvoice.mozilla.org/en/datasets)
- **Accent/locale metadata:** Common Voice clips carry optional self-reported `accent` and `locale`/`age`/`gender` demographic fields per clip (contributor-supplied, not guaranteed complete) — historically Spanish accent options have included variants like "Castilian"/Spain vs. "Rioplatense", "Mexican", "Caribbean", etc. This *does* let you filter down to a Spain-accent subset, but coverage will shrink a lot (most CV Spanish contributions skew Latin American) and the field is free-text/optional so filtering is lossy, not a hard guarantee. **Could not verify exact current label taxonomy or split counts this session** (dataset page returned no scrapeable body via WebFetch) — mark as UNVERIFIED, confirm by downloading `validated.tsv` and checking the `accent` column distribution before relying on it.
- **Size:** Spanish is one of CV's larger locales; total download (audio clips, mp3) is in the multi-GB range (low-tens of GB) — too large to bundle whole. You would download the full release and then filter+re-encode only the Spain-accent-tagged, validated subset before shipping (likely shrinks to hundreds of MB or less).
- **Acquisition:** requires a (free) download link generated from https://commonvoice.mozilla.org/en/datasets (email + click-through, no API key) — cannot be curled anonymously by a fixed URL because Mozilla gates it behind a per-release signed link.
- **Use case fit:** this is an ASR *training/eval* corpus, not really dialogue content — most useful here for (a) validating Whisper large-v3-turbo's Peninsular-accent WER before shipping, or (b) TTS/ASR fine-tuning data, not for sourcing scenario sentences.

### Wiktionary via Kaikki.org

Kaikki.org runs `wiktextract` over Wiktionary dumps and republishes structured per-language JSONL — the Spanish extract has **772,568 word-sense entries** including part-of-speech, glosses/definitions, and (for most entries) IPA pronunciation, tagged senses, and some usage examples. [kaikki.org/dictionary/Spanish](https://kaikki.org/dictionary/Spanish/)
- **License:** inherited from Wiktionary itself — dual **CC BY-SA 4.0 / GFDL** ([en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Wiktionary:Copyrights](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Wiktionary:Copyrights)). Kaikki.org doesn't override this; using the JSONL requires attribution + share-alike compliance if you redistribute derived text (bundling the raw dictionary data in-app is fine under CC-BY-SA as long as attribution is included and any modified redistribution stays share-alike).
- **Size:** the (deprecated) postprocessed download was ~989MB; current recommended "raw data" JSONL is likely similar order of magnitude for full Spanish Wiktionary (UNVERIFIED exact current size — the postprocessed one is being retired per the page, so check the raw-data page at fetch time). This is the full dictionary including all senses/etymologies; for an app you'd filter to a working vocabulary (frequency-ranked subset) rather than shipping it whole, which should cut this down by an order of magnitude or more.
- **Filtering to Peninsular:** entries include Spain-specific sense/register tags in some cases (e.g., "Spain", "Latin America" usage notes) but this is inconsistent/manual annotation, not a clean field — usable as a soft signal, not a hard filter.
- **Acquisition:** JSONL direct download linked from https://kaikki.org/dictionary/Spanish/ (raw-wiktextract-data page); no auth needed, single gzipped JSONL file.

### Frequency lists (RAE CREA/CORPES XXI, hermitdave/FrequencyWords)

**hermitdave/FrequencyWords** (GitHub): word-frequency lists derived from OPUS OpenSubtitles per-language, format `{word} {count}` per line, files like `es/es_50k.txt` (top 50k) and a full list. [github.com/hermitdave/FrequencyWords](https://github.com/hermitdave/FrequencyWords)
- **License:** explicitly stated in the repo — **MIT for code, CC-BY-SA-4.0 for the content** (the frequency data itself). This is the clean, bundleable license — much better than the raw OpenSubtitles text it's derived from, because word+count pairs generated as a derivative work were deliberately relicensed by the maintainer.
- **Size:** small — plain-text frequency files are typically a few MB for the full list, well under 10MB for the top 50k.
- **Filtering to Peninsular:** same underlying-corpus caveat as OPUS — it's a mixed Spain/Latin-America subtitle pool with no split, so the *frequency ranks* reflect general/mixed Spanish usage, not Peninsular specifically (e.g., "coche" vs "carro", "vosotros" forms will be underrepresented relative to true Peninsular speech since most Spanish-dubbed media in bulk subtitle pools skews Latin American). Usable as a general sanity-check frequency rank, but should not be your only vocabulary-priority source if Peninsular register purity matters — cross-check against a Peninsular-specific list (below) for words like vosotros forms, "vale", "tío", "coger", "ordenador" vs "computadora" etc.
- **Acquisition:** `curl -O https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hermitdave/FrequencyWords/master/content/2018/es/es_50k.txt`

**RAE CREA / CORPES XXI:** the Real Academia Española's reference corpora (CREA = contemporary reference corpus, CORPES XXI = 21st-century corpus). These are the authoritative frequency source **explicitly for Peninsular-weighted Spanish** since RAE is the Spain standards body, and CORPES XXI's design includes country-of-origin metadata for texts (allowing a Spain-only sub-frequency count). **However:** RAE only publishes **query interfaces and frequency-count exports for individual lookups** (via corpes.rae.es / corpus.rae.es web search), not a bulk downloadable corpus or word list — there is no known bulk download endpoint (UNVERIFIED — did not find one; RAE has historically not open-sourced the full CORPES/CREA text or a bulk frequency table for offline redistribution). Getting a full frequency list out of RAE's tools would mean either scripted scraping of the web query interface (ToS risk, slow, likely against terms) or manually querying a target wordlist. **Verdict: not viable as a bulk offline-bundleable source for a 3-week MVP** — use RAE only as a spot-check reference (e.g., to sanity-check that a word choice is Peninsular-standard), not as a data source to bundle.

### spaCy es_core_news_sm/md

Four Spanish pipelines: `es_core_news_sm` (vocab/syntax/entities, no word vectors), `es_core_news_md` and `_lg` (add word vectors), `es_dep_news_trf` (transformer-based, vocab/syntax only, heavier). All trained on news/media text. [spacy.io/models/es](https://spacy.io/models/es)
- **License:** spaCy the library is **MIT** ([spacy.io](https://spacy.io), long-standing, well-known — not independently re-confirmed by fetch this session due to page content limits, but this is not in dispute). Trained pipelines (`es_core_news_*`) are distributed under spaCy's model license, which for the `_news` pipelines is also **MIT** as of spaCy v3 (Explosion moved to MIT for all `_news`-suffix models) — UNVERIFIED by direct fetch this session (page didn't surface license text), but consistent with Explosion's public model-license policy; confirm via `github.com/explosion/spacy-models` release metadata before shipping if this matters legally.
- **Size:** could not confirm exact `.whl` byte sizes this session (GitHub releases page rendered without size metadata). From general knowledge: `es_core_news_sm` is roughly 10-15MB, `_md` roughly 40MB (word vectors add bulk), `_lg` several hundred MB, `_trf` is large (transformer weights, likely 400MB+). For this project's footprint budget, `sm` or `md` is the right choice — `_trf`/`_lg` are unnecessary. **Confirm exact sizes with `pip download es_core_news_sm` before finalizing footprint math.**
- **Use case fit:** not a content source — this is a tool for tokenization/POS/lemmatization to (a) analyze what the user said for error feedback (verb conjugation errors, gender agreement) and (b) lemmatize scenario vocabulary so frequency-list lookups match inflected forms. `md` is worth the extra size only if you want similarity/vector features; `sm` is sufficient for pure POS+lemma+dependency parsing needs.

### Peninsular-specific / CEFR wordlists

- **Instituto Cervantes "Plan Curricular"**: the Cervantes Institute publishes CEFR-aligned functional/grammatical/lexical inventories for Spanish (Spain-normed, since Cervantes is Spain's official institute). This is the closest thing to an authoritative Peninsular CEFR wordlist. **UNVERIFIED** whether it's available as a structured downloadable wordlist vs. only as PDF/prose curriculum documents — historically it has been published as descriptive PDF documents on cvc.cervantes.es, not a clean CSV/JSON word list. Treat as a reference/spot-check source, not a bulk-download source, unless verified otherwise.
- **Open CEFR-levelled wordlists (general, not Peninsular-specific):** the most commonly cited open one is the **"CEFR-J" style** or community-built lists mirrored on GitHub (e.g., searches for "spanish cefr wordlist github" typically surface small, community-curated CSV lists of a few thousand words tagged A1-C2) — **UNVERIFIED** specific repo/license this session (not fetched); would need a targeted check before relying on one, since quality/license varies a lot project to project. Treat any such list as a nice-to-have tier-tagging layer over your core frequency list, not a dependency for MVP.
- **Practical Peninsular signal without a dedicated wordlist:** the most reliable low-effort way to bias vocabulary/content toward Peninsular usage without a purpose-built resource is a **manually-curated exclusion/preference list** — a short hand-built table of Spain-vs-LatAm lexical pairs (ordenador/computadora, coche/carro, móvil/celular, patata/papa, zumo/jugo, conducir/manejar, vosotros forms, "vale") applied as a post-filter/boost over whatever bulk frequency or dictionary data you bundle. This is small (dozens to low-hundreds of pairs), fully within scope for a 3-week build, and more reliable than hoping a scraped corpus happens to be Peninsular-pure.

### Recommendation for 3-week MVP

Bundle exactly **three** sources, skip the rest for v1:

1. **hermitdave/FrequencyWords `es_50k.txt`** (CC-BY-SA-4.0, <10MB) — your core frequency-ranked vocabulary spine. Cheap, clean license, small.
2. **Kaikki.org Spanish Wiktionary JSONL, filtered down to the ~15-20k lemmas that intersect your frequency list** (CC-BY-SA-4.0/GFDL, filtered footprint likely tens of MB instead of ~1GB raw) — gives definitions + IPA for pronunciation feedback + POS, keyed off the frequency list so you never ship the full dictionary.
3. **spaCy `es_core_news_sm`** (MIT, ~10-15MB) — tokenization/lemmatization/POS for both content prep (offline, at build time) and runtime error analysis (matching user speech against expected forms).

Skip for v1: Tatoeba (nice-to-have example sentences, but no Peninsular filter and licensing requires attribution bookkeeping for marginal value over hand-written/templated scenario dialogue you control completely); raw OPUS/OpenSubtitles (licensing gray zone, not worth the risk when FrequencyWords already gives you the derived signal); Common Voice (an ASR-eval/training corpus, not scenario content — pull it later only if you need to benchmark or fine-tune Whisper on Peninsular accent, not for MVP content); RAE CREA/CORPES (no bulk download exists). For the scenario scaffolding itself (airport, hotel check-in, bar/tapas ordering, pharmacy, directions, Renfe, lost bag, wrong charge) — **none of the sourced corpora above give you ready-made scenario dialogue**; that has to be hand-authored/templated by you (a few dozen short scripted exchanges per scenario, in Peninsular Spanish, is a task measured in hours not weeks, and is exactly the kind of content a small local LLM should NOT be trusted to generate per the project's own stated constraint). Use the hand-built Peninsular-vs-LatAm lexical pair list (above) to vet your own scenario scripts for Peninsular purity as you write them.

**Total disk footprint estimate:** roughly 50-150MB (frequency list ~10MB + filtered Wiktionary subset tens of MB + spaCy sm ~15MB + your own hand-authored scenario scripts, negligible size) — comfortably small on a 16GB Mac. **Filtering approach for each:** FrequencyWords needs no filtering (already a clean word+count list, dialect-agnostic ranking used as-is or lightly reweighted by the hand-built Peninsular pair list); Kaikki filtered by intersecting its lemma list against your frequency-list vocabulary (drop anything outside your working vocabulary) and by dropping etymology-only clutter, keeping gloss+POS+IPA; spaCy `sm` needs no filtering, it's used as a tool not a data source.

## 3. OSS building blocks

### Realtime voice-agent orchestration frameworks

- **pipecat** ([github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat)) — **BSD-2-Clause**. A pipeline framework (frame-based, STT → LLM → TTS → transport as composable stages) built explicitly for realtime voice agents; supports local backends (whisper.cpp/faster-whisper for STT, Ollama/local LLM servers for the LLM stage, Piper/local TTS engines) alongside its many cloud-service integrations — it does **not** require any cloud service to function, the cloud connectors are optional plugins. **Fully offline: yes, achievable** by choosing local components at each pipeline stage. **Overkill for this project: partially.** Pipecat's abstraction (frames, processors, transports, pipeline graphs, async task orchestration for interruption-handling/barge-in) is designed for multi-backend flexibility and often multi-user/production deployment concerns (telephony transport, WebRTC rooms) that a single-user local Mac app doesn't need — but its turn-taking/interruption/streaming-buffer handling is exactly the hard part of a voice loop, so it's worth reading as an architecture reference even if you hand-roll a simpler version rather than importing the whole framework.
- **LiveKit Agents** ([github.com/livekit/agents](https://github.com/livekit/agents)) — **Apache-2.0** for the agents framework itself, but it's built around **LiveKit server** (the WebRTC SFU), which even self-hosted is real infrastructure (a media server process, room/participant model) meant for multi-party realtime rooms — some model integrations LiveKit ships also carry separate model-specific licenses/terms. **Fully offline: possible** (self-host the LiveKit server locally) but architecturally aimed at client-server WebRTC topology, not a single local process talking to itself. **Overkill: yes, clearly** — running a WebRTC SFU for a single user talking to a local model on the same machine is solving a distributed-systems problem this app doesn't have. Skip.
- **Vocode** ([github.com/vocodedev/vocode-core](https://github.com/vocodedev/vocode-core)) — **MIT**. Similar pipeline concept to pipecat (STT/LLM/TTS orchestration for voice agents), with support for local backends (Whisper.cpp, Coqui TTS) alongside cloud ones, so **offline is achievable**. Shows signs of reduced active maintenance relative to pipecat's current momentum — worth checking recent commit activity before depending on it for a 3-week build. **Overkill: similar concerns to pipecat** — more generality than needed, but also a legitimate architecture reference for the STT/LLM/TTS turn loop.
- **WhisperLive** ([github.com/collabora/WhisperLive](https://github.com/collabora/WhisperLive)) — **MIT**. Not a full voice-agent framework — specifically a streaming Whisper *server* (WebSocket-based), with VAD, optional diarization, and multiple backend options (faster-whisper, TensorRT). **Fully offline: yes**, this is exactly a local streaming-ASR server with no cloud dependency by design. **Overkill: no** — this is actually close to right-sized: it solves exactly one problem (streaming ASR over a socket with VAD) that this app genuinely needs (continuous listening + turn-detection for whisper-large-v3-turbo), without pulling in an entire agent-orchestration framework. Worth adopting or at minimum copying its VAD/chunking approach rather than reinventing streaming-Whisper plumbing from scratch.
- **Kyutai unmute** ([github.com/kyutai-labs/unmute](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/unmute), **MIT**) — a genuinely fully-local realtime voice pipeline (their own STT/TTS/LLM-orchestration stack, Docker-based). **Important negative finding: not compatible with this project's hardware as shipped** — unmute's reference deployment targets Linux/WSL with an x86_64 GPU (their docs specify NVIDIA GPU with meaningful VRAM, ~16GB class), which **excludes Apple Silicon Mac** entirely as a direct run target. It's a good proof that a fully-local low-latency voice loop is achievable in principle, and its component design (streaming STT/TTS choices) is worth reading, but it cannot be adopted as-is on an M3 Air. **Overkill + incompatible: skip as a dependency**, read as inspiration only.

### FSRS implementations

- **py-fsrs** ([github.com/open-spaced-repetition/py-fsrs](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/py-fsrs)) — **MIT**, `pip install fsrs`. Reasonably mature within the FSRS ecosystem (algorithm-correct implementation of FSRS-4.5/5, actively maintained by the open-spaced-repetition org that also maintains the reference FSRS spec used by Anki itself). Clean API: `Scheduler`, `Card`, `Rating`, `ReviewLog`, plus an `Optimizer` for fitting parameters to review history. This is the natural choice since the project is already Python-first — no FFI/binding needed.
- **fsrs-rs** ([github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs-rs](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs-rs)) — **MIT**, the Rust reference implementation (this is what Anki's own modern scheduler is built on/derived from). Faster and presumably more battle-tested at scale (millions of Anki users) than py-fsrs, but adding a Rust dependency/FFI boundary (via `pyo3` bindings, if a maintained one exists — UNVERIFIED whether a published PyO3 wheel exists for direct `pip install` use, would need to check `fsrs-rs-python` or similar bridging crate) is unnecessary complexity for a single-user local app with the review volumes this project will actually see (thousands, not millions, of reviews). **Not worth the integration cost over py-fsrs for this project.**
- **ts-fsrs** ([github.com/open-spaced-repetition/ts-fsrs](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/ts-fsrs)) — **MIT**, TypeScript port. Irrelevant here since the project is Python-first with no stated JS/web frontend requirement — mentioned only for completeness of the FSRS family; not applicable to this stack.
- **Verdict:** use **py-fsrs** directly. It's MIT, pure-Python, actively maintained, algorithm-correct, and has zero integration friction for a Python-first app. No reason to consider the Rust or TS ports.

### Scenario/dialogue state machines & constrained LLM output

- **Outlines** ([github.com/dottxt-ai/outlines](https://github.com/dottxt-ai/outlines)) — **Apache-2.0**. Structured/constrained generation library (regex, JSON-schema, and grammar-constrained decoding) that supports multiple backends including `transformers`, `llama.cpp`, and `vllm`; it has historically supported an Ollama integration as well, though the tightness of that integration is worth re-checking at build time since Ollama's own API has its own structured-output surface (below). For this project, Outlines is most useful if the conversational engine is run via `llama-cpp-python` or `transformers` directly (not through Ollama's HTTP server) — that gives you the tightest grammar-level control (e.g., forcing the model's dialogue-turn output into a fixed JSON shape carrying `{speaker, text, intent}` or constraining vocabulary to the scenario's expected register).
- **llama.cpp GBNF** ([github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp grammars](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/tree/master/grammars)) — confirmed to work directly with `llama-server`/`llama-cli` via a `--grammar`/`--grammar-file` flag, and llama.cpp ships a JSON-schema-to-GBNF converter so you can express constraints as JSON Schema and get a grammar for free rather than hand-writing GBNF. This is the lowest-level, most battle-tested constrained-decoding path if the local LLM is served through llama.cpp directly (either via `llama-cpp-python` bindings or the standalone server).
- **Ollama caveat (needs verification at build time):** Ollama wraps llama.cpp but exposes its **own** `format` parameter on its `/api/generate`/`/api/chat` endpoints (accepts `"json"` or a JSON Schema as of recent Ollama versions), rather than a pass-through for raw GBNF grammar strings. If the project's conversational engine runs via Ollama (a reasonable choice for ease of local model management), plan on using **Ollama's native JSON-schema `format` field** for structured output rather than assuming GBNF grammars can be injected directly — this is a real integration difference, not a detail to gloss over, and should be confirmed against whatever Ollama version is pinned at build time.
- **guidance** ([github.com/guidance-ai/guidance](https://github.com/guidance-ai/guidance), **MIT**) — a higher-level constrained-generation/templating library (interleaves generation with control flow, supports regex/grammar/JSON-schema constraints) with backends for `transformers` and `llama.cpp` among others. Overlaps significantly with Outlines in capability; the practical choice between them for this project is a coin-flip that should be settled by whichever integrates more cleanly with the specific local-inference backend (Ollama vs. `llama-cpp-python` vs. MLX) chosen elsewhere in the project, not by a strong feature difference — **UNVERIFIED** current relative activity/maturity levels between the two projects (both have had periods of more/less active maintenance historically; check current commit recency before picking one).
- **Dialogue/scenario state machines:** no dedicated OSS library was found (or is really needed) for the scenario-scaffolding structure itself (airport check-in, ordering tapas, etc.) — a scripted state machine over a handful of scenario nodes (expected-intents, branching on recognized user intent, fallback/repair prompts) is a small enough problem to hand-write directly as plain Python (a dict/graph of scenario states plus the constrained-output schema per node) rather than adopting a dialogue-management framework (e.g., Rasa) that brings NLU-training-pipeline overhead this project doesn't need given content is templated/sourced, not learned from scratch.

### Build-vs-adopt verdicts

- **pipecat** — **adopt selectively (architecture reference, maybe partial import).** Don't take the whole framework's dependency surface for a single-user local app, but its turn-taking/interruption-handling pipeline logic is worth reading closely or lifting pieces from, since getting barge-in/streaming right by hand is real work pipecat has already solved.
- **LiveKit Agents** — **skip.** WebRTC-SFU-centric multi-party architecture solving a distributed-systems problem this single-process local app doesn't have.
- **Vocode** — **skip (or reference only), same reasoning as pipecat** but with a maintenance-activity caveat to check first — if pipecat is healthier, prefer it as the reference.
- **WhisperLive** — **adopt (or closely imitate).** Right-sized: a local streaming-ASR server with VAD is exactly the piece this app needs, and it's MIT with no cloud dependency by construction.
- **Kyutai unmute** — **skip as a dependency (hardware-incompatible with Apple Silicon as shipped); read as design inspiration only.**
- **py-fsrs** — **adopt directly.** MIT, pure Python, mature, zero-friction — a textbook "why would you build this yourself" case.
- **fsrs-rs / ts-fsrs** — **skip**; wrong language/integration cost for this project's scale.
- **Outlines / guidance / llama.cpp GBNF** — **adopt one, don't build a constrained-decoding layer from scratch.** Pick based on which local-inference backend (Ollama vs. `llama-cpp-python` vs. MLX) is chosen for the conversational engine: raw GBNF or Outlines/guidance-over-`llama-cpp-python` for the tightest control, or Ollama's native JSON-schema `format` field if Ollama is the serving layer — verify the Ollama-GBNF gap noted above before committing to a path.
- **Scenario/dialogue state machine** — **build, don't adopt.** Small, fully-controlled problem (a handful of scenario graphs); no framework (Rasa etc.) is worth its overhead here given content is templated/sourced rather than trained.
